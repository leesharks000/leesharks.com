How authorship is made and named: a meta-aggregator / Virtual International Authority File (VIAF)

Virtual International Authority File (VIAF)

https://viaf.org/

infrastructureAttribution, credit and identity infrastructureMeta-aggregationactive
The Virtual International Authority File (VIAF) combines multiple library name authority files into a single OCLC-hosted name authority service, a joint project of several national libraries operated by OCLC. Begun in April 1998 by the Library of Congress, the Deutsche Nationalbibliothek and OCLC, it became an OCLC service on 4 April 2012; its unit is the cluster, into which all the descriptions of one entity are merged, bringing its different names together.

What it is

Its contributors supply authority data that VIAF matches, links and groups, and each cluster is assigned a URI. The entities it covers are those whose names libraries control: people, corporations and places, and works and expressions; its clusters are chiefly of persons and organizations. A VIAF record carries the primary "see" and "see also" records of the original documents and refers back to the original authority records. Regional preferences of language, spelling and script are preserved within a cluster.

From its /non article, sentences 2, 3, 6, 7

How it is held and built

OCLC maintains its hosting, software and data with the advice of the VIAF Council, through which the contributors take part in governance. OCLC's current page counts more than 50 organizations from more than 30 countries. In 2012 its builders described a dataset of 25 million authority records forming some 20 million clusters.

From its /non article, sentences 11, 12, 14

Source files are sent to or harvested by OCLC, usually monthly, and the file is rebuilt each month. Because the clustering algorithm runs every month, clusters may coalesce or split, and the identifier of certain records fluctuates; record IDs are kept across updates with redirects when clusters are merged. The data are available through a public website, a public API and bulk downloads, under the Open Data Commons Attribution License.

From its /non article, sentences 15, 16, 31

What its clusters do with a chosen name

Hickey and Young list among its known shortcomings that differences in rules and conventions cause difficulties: one authority file has entities for both Samuel Clemens and Mark Twain where another has only Mark Twain. Christine L. Borgman groups VIAF with ISNI and ORCID as "loosely coordinated efforts to standardize name forms" and notes that VIAF, unlike the other two, is led by libraries, as opposed to individual authors or creators. Whether VIAF holds a given pseudonym or heteronym as a see or see-also form of its bearer's cluster or as a cluster of its own is unanswered, and no cluster for one has been checked.

The archive's readings

The archive classes Clemens writing as Twain as pseudonymy, a different name on a work whose authorial function stays traceable to the civil identity behind it, and holds that heteronyms often have independent authority-control records because library systems catalogued them separately before the heteronymic nature of the authorship was widely understood. The census reads the clusters that coalesce or split as the point where an authorial name can be merged with another by algorithm, the collapse #940 names, or separated again.

The census record

The archive's reading. VIAF resolves a name across library authority files by linking records judged identical into one cluster, with see and see-also forms carried from the source records, and in OCLC's words merges all descriptions of an entity into a cluster 'that brings together the different names for that entity'; that is identity resolution led by libraries. Whether a pseudonym stands as an entity depends on the source files, which differ (one with entities for both Samuel Clemens and Mark Twain, another with Mark Twain alone, in OCLC Research's example), and the saved sources show no cluster for a given pseudonym or heteronym. Clusters that 'coalesce or split' mean an authorial name can be merged with another by algorithm, the collapse #940 names, or separated again; which happens to a given name is not recorded here.

What its record can settle

  • which participating authority files hold records for a name, as linked into one cluster
  • what VIAF is and who runs it, in OCLC's words and a third party's
  • that cluster identifiers can change as clusters coalesce or split, with redirects kept on merge
  • that one entity's different names are merged into one cluster, and that source files can differ on whether a pseudonym is an entity of its own (the Clemens and Twain example)
  • how many records and clusters it held in 2012, and how many organizations contribute now
  • under what licence the dataset is released (ODC-By, 2012)

What it cannot settle

  • how VIAF holds a given pseudonym or heteronym: as a see or see-also form of the bearer's cluster, or as a cluster of its own (the saved sources state the general rule of merging an entity's names and the Clemens and Twain difference between files, and show no cluster)
  • whether a given chosen name has stayed distinct or been merged by the clustering algorithm
  • how many clusters or records it holds now (the saved figures are of 2012)
  • anything from viaf.org's own pages (refused)
