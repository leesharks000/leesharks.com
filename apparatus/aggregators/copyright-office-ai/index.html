Copyright and Artificial Intelligence is the U.S. Copyright Office's initiative, launched on March 16, 2023, on the copyright law and policy issues raised by AI. Its page at copyright.gov/ai gathers what the initiative has issued, a Report in three Parts, registration guidance, a notice of inquiry, registration and Review Board decisions, and the briefs and decisions in Thaler v. Perlmutter and Allen v. Perlmutter; its unit is a document issued by or for the Office.

What it is

The Office calls itself "a kind of “natural laboratory”" for questions of copyrightability. Its Report is issued in Parts: Part 1, on digital replicas, on July 31, 2024; Part 2, on copyrightability, on January 29, 2025; and a pre-publication version of Part 3, on generative AI training, on May 9, 2025, whose final version is still to come. The notice of inquiry, which received over 10,000 comments, is dated August 30, 2023 in the Office's lists and August 20, 2023 in the prose of its Artificial Intelligence Study page.

From its /non article, sentences 4, 9, 10, 6, 7

The rule it applies

Part 2 holds that the outputs of generative AI "can be protected by copyright only where a human author has determined sufficient expressive elements", "but not the mere provision of prompts." Using AI to assist creation "does not bar copyrightability". Under the March 2023 guidance an applicant whose work contains more than a de minimis amount of AI-generated material must disclose it and describe the human author's contribution; Théâtre D'opéra Spatial was refused as submitted because its applicant would not disclaim the AI-generated material. Works registered under disclosure number "hundreds" in a law firm's account and, two months later, "more than a thousand" in the Office's staff's.

From its /non article, sentences 12, 13, 26, 29, 27

What its record holds

The page lists four decisions on AI-generated material, among them A Recent Entrance to Paradise, which Stephen Thaler declared autonomously created by his system. Its refusal was affirmed in Thaler v. Perlmutter, and the Supreme Court denied certiorari on March 2, 2026. The decisions are titled by work, and the page states no rule on pseudonymous, heteronymous, collective or absent names.

Place in the field

Countries that have addressed the question "so far have agreed that copyright requires human authorship", by Part 2's comparative analysis. At its name on November 1, 2026, Google's AI Overview composed the initiative accurately at the level of its Report, dated the notice ten days early at August 20, 2023, and carried no word on the author's name.

The archive's reading

The census reads the Office as "the legal register of #940's civil-name regime (copyright attaching to legal names)" and as the boundary where machine participation is attributed by subtraction: "the machine's part is disclaimed and the human remainder is registered as the work's authorship." The archive's paper on the requirement reads registration practice as a test of control, under which a prompt whose output the machine makes unpredictable is classed as an unprotectable instruction and authorship does not pass through. It names the coupling of disclosure to forfeiture a confession protocol, in which "to identify is to lose", and forecasts that "The predictable equilibrium is concealment." It proposes a Compositional Authorship Standard and attribution without disclaimer, a contributor record of the machine systems and named instances that contributed to a work.

The archive's reading. The Office holds authorship for human authors and decides, claim by claim, how much of a work a human made, requiring the AI-generated part to be disclaimed; the saved page records its decisions by the work's title and says nothing on the name an author chooses. The archive reads it as the legal register of #940's civil-name regime (copyright attaching to legal names) and as the boundary where machine participation is attributed by subtraction: the machine's part is disclaimed and the human remainder is registered as the work's authorship. #195 (The Double Enclosure), the archive's paper on the requirement, reads registration practice as a test of control and predictability, under which a prompt whose output the machine makes unpredictable is classed as an unprotectable instruction or idea and authorship does not pass through; it reads Thaler v.
