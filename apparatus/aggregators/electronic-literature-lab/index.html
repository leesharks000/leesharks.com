Electronic Literature Lab (ELL)

https://labs.wsu.edu/grigar-ell

infrastructureAvant-garde and experimental archivesactive
The Electronic Literature Lab (ELL) is a research lab and archive at Washington State University Vancouver, founded and directed by Dene Grigar, that keeps obsolete computers working so that early electronic literature, born-digital art and video games can be read on the equipment they were made for. Its history begins with an exhibition of 2008, and it built and manages The NEXT.

What it is

Its university page calls it a lab "For Advanced Inquiry into Born Digital Literature" and one of four media archaeology labs in the United States that allow scholars access to obsolete digital works on appropriate computer equipment. Its mission, in its own words, is "care for human expression and the human experience".

From its /non article, sentences 3, 5

History

Its history begins with the exhibition Grigar curated for the Electronic Literature Organization's 2008 conference. On January 10, 2011 she moved into the lab with sixteen legacy computers; Wikipedia gives 2011 as its founding year. In 2013 Grigar and Stuart Moulthrop began the Traversals, recordings of works made on historically appropriate platforms. From December 2016 the lab received the archives of trAce and Turbulence.org, and the repository that followed became The NEXT, launched on May 6, 2020. In 2021 the lab rebuilt The NEXT so that it was accessible via machine readers.

From its /non article, sentences 10, 11, 12, 13, 15, 16, 17

Holdings and conservation

Its holdings are counted differently by source and date: the university page gives 61 vintage computers dating back from 1977 and a library of 200 electronic literary works; Wikipedia gives over 80 computers serving over 3,000 works; the lab's own site gives over 100 computers. It works in four spaces: the Reading Room; the Archives Room, which holds the physical archives of The NEXT and the ELO; the Restoration Room, opened in January 2026; and the Studio.

From its /non article, sentences 24, 26

From 2019 it has restored and reconstructed works held in its archives, among them Deena Larsen's "Kanji-Kus" poems, Thomas Disch's Amnesia, as Amnesia Restored, and Stuart Moulthrop's Victory Garden. In 2020 it made its own metadata schema, ELMS 1.0.

Authors and names

The university page names a library of 200 works and lists none of their authors, and its catalogue was not saved. The lab's own site names authors throughout its history, each beside the work it traversed, restored, reconstructed, built or received. One author names a room: the Reading Room, dedicated on July 23, 2025, bears the name of "the pioneering artist Marjorie C. Luesebrink who published under the name M. D. Coverley". The NEXT's account holds 27 of the 66 works in her collection under the pen name.

Place in the field

James O'Sullivan called the lab "what is possibly the greatest collection of first-generation e-lit in the Western world". The archive's field survey places electronic literature in the procedural lineage that "established that composition can be delegated to systems". In the archive's typology a pseudonym "changes the label attached to an authorial function", traceable to the civil identity behind it.
