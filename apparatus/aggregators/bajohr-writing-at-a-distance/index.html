The archive's reading. Bajohr's two models bracket #940's question from either side: causal authorship keeps the single human author at the end of the chain, and distributed authorship disperses the work into a network that 'can no longer be addressed as an ethically responsible agent'. #940's heteronym, attributable through the civil bearer who keeps responsibility and irreducible to that bearer, sits between the two, and the essay's recorded practice (authors keep attaching their own names to works made with AI) is the civil-name concentration #940 describes; the concepts are an interlocutor's and are kept as Bajohr's own.

What its record can settle

  • how far, in operations, a human stands from a final text in a given writing configuration (by the four degrees)
  • what a network model of authorship includes (intertext, labor, programmers, companies, law, material resources) and what it loses (an ethically responsible agent)
  • how a set of computer-generated and AI-assisted works have been named and claimed, as the essay reads them

What it cannot settle

  • how a named authorial function distinct from its civil bearer is constituted: the causal model assumes a single author, and the network model dissolves the bearer of responsibility
  • any empirical question about readers, writers or credit
  • the attribution of a particular work beyond the cases it reads

Kept apart

  • NOT an account of heteronymy: its causal model assumes a single author and its network model dissolves the responsible agent
  • NOT a synonym for #940's terms: causal and distributed authorship are Bajohr's concepts, compared and kept apart
  • NOT an empirical study
  • NOT the companion essay On Artificial and Post-artificial Texts (Poetics Today, 2024), cited in note 108 and held pending in this census
