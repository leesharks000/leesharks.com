Meta-Aggregators of the Crimson Hexagon

How authorship is made and named: a meta-aggregator

A census of how authorship is made, named, distributed, attributed and sustained: works and practices made with generative models, records of the writing process, attribution and identity infrastructure, reception research on how readers judge a stated or supposed author, theories of authorship under AI, heteronymous and experimental archives, and the law and policy that draw authorship's boundary. Each resource is read from its own pages, and each states what it is to this census: a direct case, a theoretical interlocutor, an empirical comparator, a historical lineage, infrastructure, a counterposition or a peripheral comparator. Every field is quoted from a saved copy of the resource's own pages, with the date read and a hash of the copy. Compiled 2026-11-01; editor Lee Sharks.

The census asks of each resource: How is authorship made, named, distributed, attributed and sustained across persons, personas, collectives and machine participation, and what is lost when those relations are collapsed?

The question comes from EA-HETERONYMY-01 v0.2: Heteronymy as Ethical Operation Against Civil-Name Reduction (AXN:03B8, #940), which separates a distinct authorial function from the civil bearer who keeps custody and legal responsibility, and from AI Calls You Fake: The Existence Tax on Real People and Real Work (AXN:0B2A, #1722), on the existence tax laid on real people and real work. A census that kept only names would miss most of that: how a function is constituted, how contributions are assigned, and how a work goes on through other composing agents. Revised 2026-11-01 on the operator's ruling, adopting an outside selection review. The full entity pages wait for each resource's /non article.

Its sibling census, on how literature reaches a receiving apparatus and what that apparatus makes from it, is the Training-Layer Literature meta-aggregator; the family's other censuses are Labor Vector (labor and AI) and Mary Lee Labor (left politics and labor). The rest of the apparatus wing holds the archive's own provenance, rights and captures.

Copyright and Artificial Intelligence

infrastructureBoundary and governanceAttribution, credit and identity infrastructureactive
Authorial function and responsibility: human authorship as the condition of registration: in the Office's announcement of Part 2 (NewsNet Issue 1060, January 29, 2025), the outputs of generative AI 'can be protected by copyright only where a human author has determined sufficient expressive elements', which can include a human-authored work perceptible in an output or a human's creative arrangements or modifications of it, 'but not the mere provision of prompts'; the March 2023 guidance, as a law firm's account (Skadden, third party) gives it, requires an applicant whose work contains more than a de minimis amount of AI-generated material to disclose it and describe the human author's contribution; in Wikipedia's account (third party), AI-generated content beyond de minimis must be disclaimed in the application, and a claim whose applicant will not disclaim it cannot be registered; human selection, arrangement and coordination of AI outputs has been argued as sufficient human authorship for a composite work; works 'where the expressive elements are determined by a machine' remain uncopyrightable; the Office's own page records its decisions by work title

Human Authored Certification

certification: a project of the Authors Guild that lets an author certify that their book is created by a human, with a public database of certifications and a certification mark · the Authors Guild
counterpositionBoundary and governanceactive
Authorial function and responsibility: self-certification under licence: the author certifies that 'the text of the book was written by a human and not generated by AI', minimal uses excepted (spelling and grammar checks, research; the database page adds brainstorming); the FAQ, the usage guidelines and the announcement require the text to be 'fully authored by one or more human beings', a de minimis amount of AI-generated or AI-modified text excepted, a threshold the FAQ says mirrors the U.S. Copyright Office's guidance; the author represents and warrants that the work is Human Authored, and the Guild states it knows no 'truly reliable way to test whether a work includes AI-generated material' and runs 'a self-certification system with enforcement'; every certifying author signs a licensing agreement; non-members verify identity through a third-party service; the certification covers the text and is listed by title, authors and registration number
